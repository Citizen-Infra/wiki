---
title: "Om Sleiman Farm: a community-supported farm in Bil'in (Palestine)"
description: A farm in a West Bank village where members buy the season before it is planted, sharing the harvest and the risk, and where growing food is part of staying on the land.
---

Bil'in, a village west of Ramallah, is best known for its weekly protests against Israel's separation wall, which took hundreds of acres of the village's farmland in 2005. After years of protest and legal action, residents won back about half of it. On village land at the edge of that struggle, with the wall and the settlement of Modi'in Illit in view, sits **Om Sleiman Farm**: the name is Arabic for "ladybug".

The farm started in early 2016. In its own telling, it was born at the meeting of three Palestinian stories: **Mohammed Abujayyab**, who grew up in a refugee camp in Gaza and later emigrated to the US before coming back to his farming roots; **Muhab Al-Alami**, a Jerusalemite running a hostel in Ramallah and political tours to places like Bil'in; and **Abu Alaa Mansour**, a Bil'in landowner who offered them access to his land. **Yara Dowani** joined in 2018 as farm manager.

## How the farm is paid for

Om Sleiman runs as **community-supported agriculture (CSA)**. Instead of growing first and hoping to sell, the farm asks a community of households to *buy the season before it starts*; in return, members receive a weekly share of the harvest. The farm says this solved its chronic marketing and planning problems and gave it the capital to start each season. Selling directly also cuts out the middlemen who would otherwise take the farmer's margin, and it builds what the farm calls an economic relationship based on mutual trust. Members are encouraged to visit at any time, so that they see themselves as part of the production, not just as customers.

By the farm's 2021 account it was sharing its harvest with 25 families, and had gone up to 30. A 2024 report put membership at 20 to 30, depending on the season.

## More than a vegetable box

The farm treats its work as popular education. It trains farmers and activists in agroecology, natural building and running a CSA, and goes beyond teaching to hands-on support: seeds and seedlings, design help, shared markets. It saves and propagates *baladi* (local heirloom) seeds from villages including Deir Ballut, Kira and Bil'in, and from the Palestine Heirloom Seed Library. Volunteers come year-round to help with seed saving, irrigation, natural building, fermentation and planting.

None of it is apolitical. The farm is in Area C of the West Bank, under full Israeli military control, and part of its land is hard to reach safely: in 2024 a member described soldiers shooting at the team as they checked carob trees inside the farm's own boundary. Its founders describe the farm as "a declaration of agency and imagination" and a bridge between village and city, while its members talk about growing their own food as part of being free: the farm aims at independence from the occupier's economy as much as at reconnecting Palestinians with the land.

## Why it's a story worth knowing

A CSA is citizen infrastructure in its plainest form: a small group of households agrees to carry a farm's risk with the farmer, up front, in exchange for a share of what it grows. It is the farm-share version of the membership and patronage models in [funding the commons](/concepts/funding-the-commons/), and the same tool is listed in the toolkit under [fund collective work](/toolkit/fund-collective-work/). Om Sleiman shows what that arrangement can hold up beyond the money: a seed bank, a training ground and a reason to stay on contested land.
