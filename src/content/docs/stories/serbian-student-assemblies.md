---
title: "Serbia's student movement and local assemblies (2024–2026)"
description: How student plenaries, neighbourhood assemblies and practical support networks organised together before Serbia's 2026 parliamentary election.
---

In Serbia's 2024–2026 student movement, **faculty plenaries and neighbourhood assemblies gave people places to discuss problems, organise work and support one another**. They connected political education with everyday tasks: feeding students occupying faculties, collecting money, organising local actions and reaching people beyond the university cities.

Two public accounts describe this work from inside the movement. Aleksandar Matković's October 2026 essay looks back on the faculty occupations and their political consequences. A September discussion hosted by the SDi Foundation, with Agneš Asodi and Miloš Zeković, records local assembly practices and the difficulties of working across different backgrounds. These are firsthand and practitioner accounts, with different perspectives and limits.

## Learning before a common programme

Matković describes lecturing at blockaded faculties in 2024 and 2025, and being involved in the movement before moving abroad. In his account, students discussed lithium mining, economic power, healthcare and education, sometimes for six hours. Much of this happened before they had a shared political programme. He interprets that process of learning together as part of what made later organisation possible.

The plenaries were student spaces. Student participants in the SDi discussion distinguish them from neighbourhood assemblies, which were open to other residents as well as students. These local gatherings brought together people living near one another with different occupations, experiences and political views. Their participation model differs from the [randomly selected citizens' assemblies](/concepts/citizens-assembly/) described elsewhere in this wiki.

## Practical work gave people different ways to contribute

Asodi describes her local assembly cooperating with students living in occupied faculties: residents prepared food, raised money and contributed time. Zeković describes activity groups for tasks such as cooking, recycling, protecting local green space and organising attendance at protests. People could contribute through work they were able and willing to do.

The discussion also records coordination between university centres and neighbourhood groups. Student participants describe coordinators, written rules, specialised working groups and an oversight body. Organising without a single figurehead still involved roles, rules and ways to coordinate across places. The arrangements varied between university cities; the recording does not establish one uniform model for the whole movement.

## A facilitation framework did not remove conflict

Asodi and Zeković used **Spiral Dynamics**, a framework for discussing different values, in a short educational programme for assembly participants. They describe introducing the framework, inviting people to reflect on their own patterns, and discussing a group profile before applying it to a practical activity. Their aim was to help neighbours understand different ways of contributing and communicating.

They also report limits. Recognising different values did not necessarily give people the communication skills to work together. Where resentment was already deep, the new language could become another way to attack an opponent. These observations come from the practitioners' account; the recording does not provide an independent evaluation showing that the programme improved assembly effectiveness. The case connects to [working with conflict](/concepts/working-with-conflict/) and [learning across a network](/concepts/learning-across-a-network/).

## The move into parliamentary politics remained a test

After the parliamentary election of **25 October 2026**, Serbia's election commission reported the Student List ahead of the incumbent list in its preliminary results. Matković's essay, published two days after the vote, asks how a movement with different ideological strands can carry its collective methods into parliament, where it must also nominate people for formal leadership roles.

The election result does not by itself answer that question. These sources document practices developed before the vote and questions raised immediately afterwards. They do not establish how the resulting parliamentary group will govern or whether local assemblies will gain lasting influence over public decisions. For the wider distinction between organising a movement and securing an institutional response, see [movements and arguments](/concepts/movements-not-arguments/) and [binding or advisory participation](/concepts/binding-vs-advisory/).
