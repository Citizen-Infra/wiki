---
title: The commons (and why it's not a tragedy)
description: A commons isn't a free-for-all that inevitably gets wrecked. It's one of the oldest and most practical ways humans manage shared wealth — and the famous "tragedy" was a misunderstanding.
---

A **commons** happens wherever people come together to manage some shared wealth — a fishery, a forest, a piece of code, a mutual-aid network — with fairness, participation, and an eye to the long term, and organise to defend it against being privatised or captured. David Bollier likes to stress that "commons" is really a *verb*: **commoning**, the ongoing practice of stewarding something together.

It's easy to assume the commons is archaic — peasants and village pastures. In fact commons are entirely contemporary: urban spaces, digital commons, open-source software, community land trusts, time banks, mutual aid. A commons can happen almost anywhere people decide to manage collective wealth for collective benefit.

## The "tragedy" was never about a commons

In 1968 Garrett Hardin popularised the phrase *"the tragedy of the commons"* — the idea that any shared resource will inevitably be overused and destroyed, because each individual grabs as much as they can. It became conventional wisdom. It's also a misreading.

What Hardin described — an open pasture anyone can wreck, with no rules and no community — **isn't a commons at all.** A real commons has a *bounded community*, *rules*, and *consequences for breaking them*. What he actually described is closer to the tragedy of the **market**: an unmanaged free-for-all that invites people to behave like, in Bollier's phrase, "libertarian jerks." Elinor Ostrom later won a Nobel Prize for documenting how real commons, all over the world and across millennia, govern themselves perfectly well (see the [Ostrom Workshop](/ecosystem/overview/)).

## It's a dimmer switch, not a template

There's no single blueprint. A commons varies with what's being stewarded — land and water can be used up; digital code can be copied for free. And "commons" isn't a yes/no label but a **dimmer switch**: more or less participation, more or less bottom-up legitimacy. (A mafia or a gang shares resources too, but top-down and coercively — so it's at the dark end of the dial.) Bollier even prefers a different word for what's being stewarded: not a "resource" but **care-wealth** — something a community tends for mutual benefit.

This is the foundation under the rest of the commons concepts here: [enclosure](/concepts/enclosure/) (how commons get taken), [the OntoShift](/concepts/the-ontoshift/) (the mindset commoning requires), [relational property](/concepts/relational-property/), and the practical question of [funding the commons](/concepts/funding-the-commons/). It also sits alongside the [Commoning Standard](/concepts/commoning-standard/)'s view of self-governance as a learnable literacy.

## Make the boundary and the review process visible

Amanda Kiessel's [community-standards oral history](https://protocol.ecologies.info/interviews/kiessel-good_market/) describes **Good Market** as shared infrastructure for enterprises and networks that prioritise people and the planet. Its published [governance model](https://www.goodmarket.global/info/community-rules/governing-the-commons/) separates public access to information from participation by approved enterprises. Those enterprises can propose rule changes; networks can add their own criteria to a common baseline.

Its [directory policy](https://www.goodmarket.global/info/community-rules/directory-policy/) also makes approval revisable. Application claims appear on profiles, people with evidence can flag false claims, and a curation team reviews them. Review may lead to corrections, other action or removal. A listing under the shared minimum standards is not the same thing as every additional certification a network may offer.

This is a documented arrangement to examine, not an independent finding that every review is fair or every enterprise meets its claims. For a commons, the useful questions are concrete: **who can participate, who can change rules, what is visible, and what happens when someone challenges a claim?** The [coordination toolkit](/toolkit/coordinate-a-community/#find-groups-with-shared-standards) links the resource.

Knowledge can be stewarded through similar explicit choices. The [personal and team wiki experiment](/concepts/learning-across-a-network/#a-federation-of-personal-and-team-wikis) at Dark Matter Labs and xCO separates permission to share, attributed disagreement and group validation. Its account also records uneven adoption; governance rules and a connected system do not by themselves demonstrate shared learning.
