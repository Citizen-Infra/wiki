---
title: Framing questions
description: How to design guiding questions for dialogue, deliberation and decisions without closing down what people can say.
---

A guiding question directs a group's attention. It signals what matters, whose concerns belong in the room and which answers seem possible. **Framing questions is part of designing the process**, with consequences for participation and power.

This guide draws on Catherine Barnes's *Framing Questions for Dialogue and Deliberation*. Her recommendations come from theory and practitioner experience. They offer ways to prepare a conversation; wording alone cannot guarantee agreement or resolve a conflict.

## Start by listening

Before settling the main question, talk with people who occupy different positions in the situation. Barnes recommends asking about their concerns, hopes, understanding of other groups and the conditions that would make participation possible. Include people whose experience or power differs from the convenors'.

Listen for words that mean different things to different people. Ask what a charged term means to the person using it. These early conversations also raise expectations and affect trust, so explain what you are exploring and avoid promising an outcome you cannot deliver.

Then agree the purpose: **what do we want to change through this process?** The guiding question should make that purpose understandable while leaving people room to question assumptions and explore alternatives. Be clear about [what participants can actually influence](/concepts/binding-vs-advisory/).

## Match the question to the job

Barnes distinguishes three complementary kinds of work. The example prompts below are adapted from her report.

| Job | What the question invites | Example prompt |
|---|---|---|
| **Dialogue** | Share experience and understand why something matters, including when disagreement remains. | What experience has shaped how you see this issue? |
| **Deliberation** | Explore tensions, assumptions and possible responses together. | How can we protect public health while also respecting individual freedom? |
| **Collaborative decision-making** | Develop options, agree assessment criteria and weigh trade-offs towards action. | What criteria should we use to assess our options? |

These are different jobs, not a compulsory script for every meeting. Keep the purpose visible and revise the questions as people learn. A group may discover that it needs to explore another question before it can address the original one.

## Keep the framing open

A question can assume the answer before anyone speaks. Asking how to stop opposition to a policy treats opposition as the problem. Asking what concerns and hopes shape people's responses leaves room to understand what is at stake.

Barnes suggests using **both/and questions** to hold competing concerns in view. This can help people explore possibilities beyond a forced choice. It does not establish that every tension has a solution that satisfies everyone.

Balance problem analysis with questions about existing strengths and desired futures. What has worked well? What would people like to make possible? Later, ask which options could serve those aims and how the group would judge them.

## Make room for safe participation

- Agree how people will listen and respond before inviting sensitive stories.
- Let participants choose how much personal experience to disclose.
- Ask about the reasons behind a view with genuine curiosity. A blunt “why?” can sound like an accusation.
- Check whether the wording privileges one group's account of the problem.
- Revisit the question when new concerns or insights emerge.
