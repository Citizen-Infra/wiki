title: Community power in health
description: Questions residents can use to examine who shapes neighbourhood health plans, controls resources and holds services accountable.
---

Who gets to shape a neighbourhood health plan: the people who live there, or only the institutions providing services? **Community power in health** asks what residents can decide, what resources they control and whether services support the relationships and activity already present in a place.

Cormac Russell and Lisa McNally develop this argument in *Putting the Neighbourhood Back into Neighbourhood Health*, hosted by [New Local](/ecosystem/overview/), and four accompanying question guides. These are a provocation and practical discussion resources, not an evaluation proving health outcomes. Community activity and professional care have different roles; neighbours should not be expected to take on clinical responsibilities.

## Start with what people already bring

The authors use **Asset-Based Community Development (ABCD)** to look beyond a list of service gaps. They invite residents to identify local skills, associations, institutional resources, physical and natural spaces, reciprocal exchange, and shared history and stories.

Their proposed sequence asks what residents can do together, what they can do with support, and what they need institutions to provide. This also requires attention to unequal resources and exclusion. It is not a reason to leave communities without professional services or material support.

## Five questions for a residents' group

These prompts are adapted from the community leaders' and connectors' guide:

1. **Whose priorities?** Are residents helping set the direction before a plan is drafted, or only responding afterwards?
2. **What decision power?** Which choices can residents and local groups change or reject? Ask about actual authority, not just a seat at a meeting.
3. **Who controls resources?** What funding can the community direct, and can small or informal groups access it without a complex bidding process?
4. **Whose relationships and voices?** How will services support local groups and gathering places, reach excluded people and avoid turning volunteers into unpaid service deliverers?
5. **What comes back?** Who defines success, and how will services show what changed because residents participated?

Use these as starting questions, not a score that certifies a programme. For the distinction between consultation and authority, see [binding or advisory participation](/concepts/binding-vs-advisory/). For preparing the conversation itself, see [framing questions](/concepts/framing-questions/).

The guides address a UK health-policy context and use specific health-service and local-government roles. Adapt the questions to local institutions; they do not establish legal rights or obligations in every place.

## What would count as progress?

Look for concrete changes in decision rights, accessible resources, inclusion and feedback to residents. A programme's name or a claim to be “community-led” does not establish those things. Nor does this question set prove clinical effectiveness, cost savings or the success of any particular local programme.

This connects health-service design to [durable civic infrastructure](/concepts/durable-civic-infrastructure/): relationships, spaces, skills and resources that communities can keep using between individual projects.
