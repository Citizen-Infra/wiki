---
title: "Open Space: an agenda set by whoever shows up"
description: A meeting method with no prepared programme. Participants propose the sessions, host them, and move freely between them, in a room or across a whole diaspora online.
---

Most conferences arrive with a programme: speakers, panels, an audience that listens. **Open Space Technology** starts with only a broad theme and an invitation. Anyone who has something to say or ask puts a session on the shared board, hosts it, and the agenda is whatever the participants bring. The organisational consultant **Harrison Owen** developed it in the mid-1980s, reportedly after noticing that people valued the coffee breaks at his conferences more than the formal sessions.

It suits questions that nobody owns and nobody can answer alone: where the knowledge is spread across many people, and the point is to find each other as much as to find an answer.

## Four principles and one law

Open Space is usually explained through four principles and a single law:

- **Whoever comes are the right people.** Headcount is not the measure; the people in the conversation are the ones it needed.
- **Whatever happens is the only thing that could have.** Let go of the imagined "correct" script.
- **Whenever it starts is the right time.**
- **When it's over, it's over.** If a topic runs out early, move on; if it keeps going, don't cut it off.
- **The law of two feet.** If you are neither learning nor contributing where you are, go somewhere you can. That is a form of respect for your own and other people's time, not rudeness.

Facilitators do not lead the conversations. They **hold the space**: helping people shape a session idea, sorting out the logistics, and making sure everyone can take part. The content belongs to the participants.

:::caution
The law of two feet gives everyone an easy exit, and that includes people who disagree. If a group needs to hear the dissenting voice rather than let it walk away, pair Open Space with a method built for that, such as [deep democracy](/concepts/deep-democracy/) or the approaches in [working with conflict](/concepts/working-with-conflict/).
:::

## Open Space at a distance: Ukrainians across locations

The Ukrainian non-profit **Support Ukraine Now** (SUN) runs Open Space online. Its autumn 2026 (un)conference, **"Разом на відстані" (Together at Distance)**, is aimed at Ukrainians wherever they are: abroad (whenever and however they left), internally displaced, under occupation, in Ukraine, and second- and third-generation diaspora. Its framing question is how to remain Ukrainian when you are scattered, with an answer the organisers say "is in ourselves", not in a single document.

The [organiser's page](https://supportukrainenow.org/together-at-distance) and [format guide](https://supportukrainenow.org/together-at-distance/format) show how the method adapts when people cannot share a room:

- **A shared calendar replaces the session board.** The whole programme lives in one public Luma calendar. Anyone, an individual or an organisation, can add an event to it; organisers will add it for people who'd rather not set up an account. Each person enters times in their own time zone and the calendar converts them for everyone else.
- **Zoom rooms are kept small,** 7 to 10 people, so each person has room to speak, with local in-person meetups in some cities running alongside.
- **A session doesn't have to be a talk.** The guide lists an online conversation, an offline meetup, a survey or shared research that runs for weeks and is discussed at a live session, a game, a workshop, or a series of meetings on one topic.
- **Start from your own need.** The guide suggests proposing a session by asking what a global community of Ukrainians could do for *you*, and being clear about what you want to come away with: an idea, feedback, partners, an answer.
- **Set the frame of the space explicitly.** Events run in Ukrainian and English, under a code of conduct, and the organisers ask hosts not to use Russian-language content, music included. Open Space leaves the agenda open; it doesn't leave the ground rules open.

The event runs September to October 2026 and lists 21 partner organisations. Its stated scale ("500+ participants, 15+ countries", with "18+ countries" elsewhere on the same page) is the organiser's own figure, not an independently verified outcome, and the process was still running when this page was written.

### What earlier editions reported

SUN has run the format twice before, and published its own reports. **"Youth of Ukraine – NOW WHAT?"** ran online from April to June 2025 as part of SUN's youth leadership programme in Cherkasy, supported by the European Endowment for Democracy. The [report](https://supportukrainenow.org/now-what) counts 330+ registered participants, 32 events and about 50 hours of content. Participants valued live contact, new acquaintances and discovering the Open Space format itself; the organisers' own list of things to improve included a more focused timeline, deeper participant engagement and more personalised invitations. A second edition, **"Youth at a Crossroads"** (November 2025), lists 20 sessions in its [report](https://supportukrainenow.org/youth-at-crossroads), with feedback from 16 participants.

These are self-reports from the organiser. They are useful as a practitioner's account of what an online Open Space takes, not as an evaluation of its effects.

## Where it fits

Open Space is one way to [decide together](/toolkit/decide-together/#plan-and-facilitate-a-conversation), and a particularly good one for people who share a situation but not a place. It is a cousin of the small-group formats behind [civic listening](/concepts/civic-listening/), but with no fixed questions: the participants supply the questions too. Like a [citizens' assembly](/concepts/citizens-assembly/), it trusts ordinary people with the substance; unlike one, it makes no attempt at a representative sample and produces no collective recommendation unless the participants decide to.
