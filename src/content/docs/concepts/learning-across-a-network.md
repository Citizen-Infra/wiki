---
title: Learning across a network
description: How people turn scattered experience into shared understanding, practical tools and new experiments.
---

A network can hold a great deal of knowledge without making it useful to its participants. Experience stays in people's memories, project records and meeting notes. **Learning across a network** means bringing people together to interpret that material, turn it into something others can use, and share what happens when they try it.

In *What Does a Network Know?*, Brendon Johnson describes how the [Fito Network](/ecosystem/overview/) is trying to do this. He presents four kinds of practice and a proposed learning loop. His account offers concrete examples; it does not establish that one process will work for every network.

## Four kinds of shared work

### Interpret experience together

Fito's Network Researchers compared information about different networks and asked what their differences might reveal: how they organise, govern themselves and change over time. Johnson's point is that a database gains value through what a diverse group can notice and question together. Collecting more records is only one part of the work.

### Turn experience into usable tools

The **Network Weaving Game** grew from people comparing their experience of facilitation, governance, learning and relationships. Its public site offers reflection questions about weaving skills, followed by planning and practice resources. It is linked from [the coordination toolkit](/toolkit/coordinate-a-community/). Treat it as a learning resource; the cited pages do not establish it as a validated measure of network effectiveness.

### Notice emerging questions

Johnson describes the Networks Festival as a way to bring scattered questions, needs, failures and experiments into conversation. The useful signals can be patterns across sessions: something several groups are struggling with, or an idea that others could test. This is a practitioner account of collective sensing, not a representative survey of a whole field.

### Make the wider field visible

In Fito's Philanthropy Playground, people working on different forms of funding reform compared their perspectives. The aim was to see overlapping effort, different language for similar goals, gaps and concentrations of power. A shared picture can give people a starting point for coordination. The account does not establish that the exercise changed funding decisions.

## Keep learning in circulation

Johnson calls Fito's “Loom” a **working proposal**. Its cycle moves from experience to shared interpretation, then to tools and questions, collaborative experiments, and learning that returns to the wider field.

For a community trying this, the article raises useful questions:

- Does the research give something back to the people contributing knowledge?
- Whose experience is missing from the conversation?
- Can people adapt the resulting tools across cultures and places?
- Who will help the learning travel after the gathering ends?

The proposal's test is still practical: what do people use, adapt and learn from? Its planned research on sustaining networks should be assessed through the resulting evidence, not treated as a completed finding.

## Related ideas

This is part of the work of maintaining a [knowledge commons](/concepts/the-commons/) and building [self-governance skills](/concepts/commoning-standard/). It can complement [conversation networks](/concepts/conversation-networks/), whose focus is connecting small-group conversations, and [locality-to-locality spreading](/concepts/locality-to-locality-spreading/), where communities adapt tools and return learning to a shared pool.
