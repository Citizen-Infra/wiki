---
title: Modular politics
description: A vision of online platforms where communities assemble their own governance from interchangeable parts — instead of being stuck with one hard-coded model.
---

If [implicit feudalism](/concepts/implicit-feudalism/) is the problem — online spaces hard-wired for "admin controls all" — **modular politics** is one answer. It's a framework, developed by a group at [Metagov](/ecosystem/overview/), for building platforms that let communities **compose their own governance** out of interchangeable parts.

## Governance you cobble together

Instead of baking one form of governance into the software, a modular platform offers a base layer and a kit of governance **modules** that a community can mix and match for its own context. You might combine an executive role with a referendum that can check it; another community might wire things up completely differently. The aim is software that's as **neutral and inviting as possible** to diverse, culturally-specific ways of governing — rather than quietly imposing one (usually the admin-on-top model, or the assumption that, say, voting is always right).

This isn't only theory. Where online governance has actually been built for real stakes — Barcelona's [Decidim](/toolkit/decide-together/) platform, or blockchain governance tools like Aragon and OpenZeppelin — projects have repeatedly **converged on something modular**, often after starting with a single rigid design and finding it too limiting.

## Making governance legible

Flexible governance also needs clear descriptions of the choices a community has made. **Governance Cards**, a related research prototype from Princeton's CROW project in collaboration with [Bonfire](/ecosystem/overview/), explore a structured way to document infrastructure, membership and rules. A rule-selection interface tested with Bonfire lets communities express qualifications as well as broad categories. For example, a prototype screenshot distinguishes allowing AI-generated media, prohibiting it, requiring labelling or requiring approval.

The authors also identify a privacy problem: governance information can be sensitive. Their proposed JSON-LD format and different visibility levels are development directions, not an established interoperability or privacy guarantee. A card describes an arrangement; it does not establish that the arrangement is followed or gives members meaningful power. The [coordination toolkit](/toolkit/coordinate-a-community/#document-community-governance) records the project's prototype status.

## A covenant needs a working process

Coraline Ada Ehmke describes **Contributor Covenant** as making a community's values and expectations explicit. In her [oral history](https://protocol.ecologies.info/interviews/ehmke-contributor_covenant/), she also warns that routine adoption can weaken the signal that leaders have actively committed to those values. Maintaining the document and putting it into practice are continuing responsibilities.

The current [Contributor Covenant 3.0](https://www.contributor-covenant.org/version/3/0/) asks communities to supply reporting details and review enforcement arrangements. Its suggested responses depend on human judgment; they are not a substitute for deciding who has authority, how conflicts of interest are handled or how people can challenge a decision. Nor does a shared code by itself transfer control from an administrator to members. The [coordination toolkit](/toolkit/coordinate-a-community/#set-expectations-and-a-way-to-address-harm) links the adaptation resources.

## Why it matters

Modular politics reframes the question from "what's the right way to govern an online community?" to "how do we build tools that let *every* community find its own?" It's a direct attempt to make flexible, creative self-governance a standard feature of online life. (Schneider's more recent work asks whether an AI agent reading a plain-language constitution could do this even more flexibly — see [constitutional agents](/concepts/constitutional-agents/).)
