---
title: Working with conflict
description: Helping people address difficult issues without making quick agreement, enforced calm or a claim of neutrality the whole goal.
---

A community can look calm because people have worked through a disagreement—or because the people with least power have stopped speaking. **Working with conflict** starts by asking what people need to address, not simply how to make the disagreement disappear.

In his Protocol Oral History interview, mediator and author **Bernard Mayer** describes his work as **conflict engagement**. Sometimes people need an agreement; sometimes they need to name a difficult issue, understand a continuing disagreement, organise, or find support. His aim is to help people have the conversation they need, rather than impose his preferred solution.

## Agreement is not the only useful outcome

Mayer warns that rushing to resolution can leave an unjust or unstable situation intact. He argues that bringing underlying concerns into the conversation may matter more than lowering its emotional intensity. This does not mean provoking a confrontation, tolerating abuse or assuming every conflict can be solved through dialogue.

With Jacqueline N. Font-Guzmán, he develops the relationship between connecting across differences and changing underlying systems in *The Neutrality Trap*. Their [published excerpt](https://ssir.org/books/excerpts/entry/from_disruption_to_engagement) is an argument about social change, not evidence that one conversation format produces it.

## Be clear about the helper's role

Mayer distinguishes third-party, organisational/system and ally roles. A mediator helping parties make their own decisions is not doing the same job as an advocate supporting one party. Make the role, commitments and limits clear; do not quietly switch roles while people believe they are in a different process.

His criticism of neutrality is not a licence to manipulate people. The **2005 Model Standards of Conduct for Mediators**, adopted by the American Arbitration Association, American Bar Association and Association for Conflict Resolution, separately require **party self-determination and impartiality**. They describe voluntary, uncoerced choices about participation and outcomes, and freedom from favouritism, bias or prejudice. They also address competence, conflicts of interest, confidentiality and safety. These are professional model guidelines, not automatically the law in every jurisdiction.

## Questions before bringing people together

- **What is needed?** An agreement, a better understanding, a way to keep working together, independent advice, or an advocate?
- **Can people participate freely?** Can they decline, pause or leave, and can they understand the process and its consequences?
- **How does power enter?** Who sets the agenda, interrupts, controls resources or can impose costs on someone else? What support or process changes might be needed?
- **What stays private?** Agree on confidentiality and its limits rather than assuming that a conversation or its recording is safe to share.
- **What would count as progress?** Do not judge the work only by whether everyone signs an agreement or stops expressing anger.

These questions are a starting point, not mediation training. Violence, abuse or an inability to participate safely may require postponing or ending a process and seeking appropriate specialist help; a facilitator cannot guarantee safety or substitute for legal or clinical support.

## Connect it to community governance

Groups can make room for disagreement in their [rules and roles](/concepts/modular-politics/) before a crisis. [Framing questions](/concepts/framing-questions/) can help prepare a conversation, while [Deep democracy](/concepts/deep-democracy/) describes a distinct approach to working with minority views and tension. They are related resources, not interchangeable methods.

The [coordination toolkit](/toolkit/coordinate-a-community/#prepare-for-difficult-conversations) links here. A charter or software tool alone does not determine whether people can raise concerns, obtain support or challenge a decision.
