---
title: Relational property
description: What if owning something didn't mean absolute control over it, but a set of relationships and responsibilities agreed with a community?
---

A lot of what makes [commoning](/concepts/the-commons/) hard comes down to one inherited idea: **property as absolute dominion**. In the Western legal tradition descended from John Locke, to own a thing is to have exclusive, near-total control over it — "this is mine, that's yours." David Bollier argues that frame quietly imposes a whole set of social relationships and individual identities, ones that make it hard to think relationally about each other's needs at all.

## A different frame

Bollier and Silke Helfrich call the alternative **relationalised property**. Instead of absolute ownership with hard walls, property becomes a set of **porous boundaries**: under terms agreed by a community, you may use something — without claiming exclusive dominion over it. This isn't exotic. Indigenous, traditional, and many global-south communities already treat property this way; from their vantage point, the Lockean version can look frankly strange.

## You can hack the law toward it

You don't have to wait for the whole legal system to change. Bollier points to **legal hacks** — clever uses of existing law that open a different kind of relationship among people making things together:

- **Creative Commons licences** and the **GPL** (free/open-source software) use copyright *against* enclosure, guaranteeing that shared work stays shareable. (This wiki is itself under Creative Commons — a small legal hack.)
- **Community land trusts** hold land in trust so it stays affordable and locally governed, out of the speculative market.
- Initiatives like **A Land for All** in Israel/Palestine try to reframe a bitter conflict away from "one nation or two" toward *sharing* a land — a relational rather than exclusive claim.

## Restoration involves relationships and resources

In his [restorative-justice oral history](https://protocol.ecologies.info/interviews/williams-restorative_justice/), Rick Williams, who identifies himself as Oglala Lakota and Cheyenne, describes relationships to land through use, cultural life and obligations to others. He calls for opportunities for displaced people to return to their homelands, maintain community life and participate in stewardship. His proposals include cultural spaces, resources and co-management—not simply a different vocabulary for ownership.

This is Williams's situated account and argument, not a description of all Indigenous traditions or a legal finding about particular titles. Nor should his use of “restorative justice” be reduced to a ready-made dispute-resolution exercise. It asks what material and institutional changes could repair historical dispossession, with the affected peoples involved in defining them.

Williams also urges listeners to investigate records rather than simply believe him. One specific institutional action can be checked: Colorado's [official 2021 executive-order index](https://www.colorado.gov/governor/2021-executive-orders) lists **B 2021-002**, dated **17 August 2021**, rescinding the 1864 proclamations issued by Territorial Governor John Evans. That verifies the recorded rescission, not every legal interpretation in the interview or the completion of broader restoration proposals.

For communities considering a response, the distinction is between acknowledging a relationship and changing the resources, access or decision rights that sustain it. [People of the Sacred Land](/ecosystem/overview/) publishes related research and recommendations; their existence does not establish that those recommendations have been implemented.

## A market-based cousin: self-assessed property

Some thinkers reach a similar destination through markets rather than community custom. The 19th-century economist Henry George observed that most of a plot's value comes from the *community* around it, not its owner — an empty Manhattan would be nearly worthless. The **SALSA / Harberger** mechanism builds on that: you self-assess what your possession is worth, pay a fee on that figure, and must stand ready to sell at that price, with the proceeds recycled to the community that creates the value. It lands on the same idea this page is about — possession as **stewardship**, not absolute dominion — but arrives from the [plurality](/concepts/plurality/) / RadicalxChange direction. See [plural mechanisms](/concepts/plural-mechanisms/).

Relational property is the legal-and-cultural underside of the [commons](/concepts/the-commons/), a counter to [enclosure](/concepts/enclosure/), and a close cousin of [protocol](/concepts/protocol/) — coordination by shared, adaptable agreement rather than top-down control. For practical vehicles, see [Fund collective work](/toolkit/fund-collective-work/).
