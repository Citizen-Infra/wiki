---
title: Participation as a means, not an end
description: A common failure of civic-tech thinking is to treat "more participation" as automatically good. A pragmatist reframe from Latin America — participation as a means to specific democratic ends — is a sharper way to judge whether an innovation actually works.
---

Enthusiasm for citizen participation can slip into treating it as a good in itself: run enough consultations, build enough deliberative platforms, and democracy is somehow strengthened. The political scientist **Thamy Pogrebinschi** — whose LATINNO project catalogued three decades of democratic innovation across eighteen Latin American countries — argues this gets the logic backwards. Participation is a **means**; democracy is the end. Whether a given innovation was worth the effort depends on what it was trying to *do* for democracy — and whether it did it.

## The four means and the five ends

Pogrebinschi organises the field with a deliberately pragmatic grid. Any democratic innovation combines one or more **means** — participation via *deliberation*, *citizen representation*, *digital engagement*, or *direct voting* — to serve one or more **ends**, the qualities of democracy people are trying to improve: *accountability*, *responsiveness*, *the rule of law*, *political inclusion*, and *social equality*.

The grid does two useful things. It **stops us conflating** the mechanism with the good it might produce — a participatory budget is not intrinsically better than a deliberative forum; it depends on which democratic deficit each is answering. And it **makes failure legible**: an innovation with the right means for the wrong end (or a right end that never materialises) can be recognised as a miss, rather than defended as "engagement" per se.

## The Latin American story

The reframe grew out of an unusual empirical vantage. LATINNO tracked *thousands* of innovations across the region from 1990 to 2020, in the years when Latin America was arguably the world's most active laboratory for democratic experiment: the return of democracies, the constitutional openings of the 1990s, decentralisation, digitalisation, and above all the **"left turn"** of the 2000s in which several governments invested heavily in national-scale deliberative and participatory innovations. From roughly 2015 the wave receded — the left turn ebbed, authoritarianism grew, and the deliberative surge fell "impressively," while digital-engagement innovations rose sharply.

That trajectory changes what the region's story teaches. It is not the tidy narrative of a global "deliberative wave"; it is a warning about **fragility**: many of the most striking innovations were tied to the state that built them and did not survive the change of coalition — [Brazil's participatory budgeting](/toolkit/decide-together/) programmes being the canonical case. Which puts the question that concludes Pogrebinschi's book alongside a related worry from the political theorist Mark Warren: *what would it take to future-proof democratic innovations against authoritarian backsliding? What defensive role could they play?*

:::tip[The move the pragmatist frame makes]
Stop asking "did more people take part?" Ask which democratic problem the innovation was meant to fix — and whether, on that specific end, it moved anything at all.
:::

## Why it fits here

This is the intellectual frame under which several regional actors this wiki already tracks — [Delibera Brasil](/ecosystem/overview/), [DemoLab](/ecosystem/overview/) in Colombia, the wider [Democracy R&D](/ecosystem/overview/) network — sit together. It is also the antidote to [cargo-cult democracy](/concepts/cargo-cult-democracy/) (mistaking the *artifact* for the thing it was meant to produce) and a sharper cousin of the [institutionalising deliberation](/concepts/institutionalising-deliberation/) argument (survival is not the same as embedding; embedding is not the same as delivering the democratic end).
