---
title: Attention & democratic power
description: Who gets heard, who sets the rules of visibility, and why feeds and chatbots make those democratic questions.
---

A neighbourhood group can have a well-supported proposal and still struggle to get anyone to notice it. Public attention helps decide which problems reach an agenda and which people can build support. The rules for distributing it therefore matter to democracy.

In his Informational Democracy essays, social scientist **Petter Törnberg** argues that we should examine how media shape the competition to be heard. Asking whether a feed changes an individual voter's opinion captures only part of that power. Politicians, journalists and movements also adapt to what earns visibility: a system can reward a provocative style without changing every viewer's beliefs.

## Three questions about attention

Törnberg proposes a **distributive politics of attention**:

- **Who receives it?** Which people, problems and claims enter public view, and which remain hard to find?
- **Who allocates it?** Who chooses the ranking rules, measures success and can change the system?
- **Who bears the costs?** Who deals with harassment, misleading claims or damage to shared institutions when those help someone else gain reach?

His proposal does not require equal airtime for every claim. Some issues deserve more scrutiny; some people need protection from unwanted exposure. The aim is to make the rules publicly contestable, rather than leaving their objectives entirely to platform owners.

This expands the question behind [bridging & pro-social media](/concepts/bridging-and-pro-social-media/). A better ranking measure can help, while who governs the measure and who can challenge its effects remain questions of power. Tang's [community-control approach](/concepts/six-pack-of-care/) asks related questions about mandate, answerability and the ability to stop a system.

## From a public feed to a private answer

In *After Social Media*, Törnberg distinguishes two directions for media power:

- **Algorithmic broadcasting.** A model predicts what will keep each person watching. Social relationships become one input to selection, and generative AI can supply material without relying on users to make it.
- **Chatbots as media.** The person asks a question, while the system supplies an interpretation in a private exchange. Other citizens may never see the answer, know whether theirs differs, or have an opportunity to challenge it publicly.

These are his analysis of emerging media forms, rather than a claim that every platform has completed the transition. His warning is that centralised selection can persist while shared public experience weakens. A visible post can be disputed; a privately tailored answer is harder for others to inspect. This connects to [orphan reasons](/concepts/orphan-reasons/): who answers when a machine's words shape a consequential decision?

## What remains open

The essays set out a problem and questions for institutional design. They do not demonstrate that a particular allocation rule produces fairer politics. Törnberg's planned third essay addresses democratically governed attention infrastructure; as of 18 October 2026, it is not in the publisher's public archive.
