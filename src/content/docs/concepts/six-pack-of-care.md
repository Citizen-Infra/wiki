title: The 6-Pack of Care
description: Audrey Tang and Caroline Green's test for AI a community can actually trust — not one system built to govern everyone, but many small local stewards it can own, inspect, correct, and switch off.
---

Most debates about "aligning" AI to human values try to solve it from the top down: write better rules, infer better preferences, train better models. **Civic AI**, as Audrey Tang and Caroline Green use the term, starts somewhere else. It asks who gets heard, who is accountable, how failures are repaired, and when a system should be switched off — and it answers by building **many small AIs a community can own, inspect, correct, and shut down**, rather than one powerful system built to govern everyone.

Each of these bounded local stewards has a name in their framework — a **Kami** — a spirit of a particular place, not a universal governor. The **6-Pack of Care** is the test for whether one can be trusted.

## The six packs

The first four form a loop — the *care cycle* — that keeps a system answerable to the people it affects. The last two make that loop hold across many organisations and over time.

1. **Attentiveness** — noticing what the people closest to a problem see that institutions miss.
2. **Responsibility** — who is accountable, with what authority, and what happens if they fail.
3. **Competence** — whether the system actually works in practice: audited, explainable, and safe to fail.
4. **Responsiveness** — whether affected people can contest an outcome and force a repair.
5. **Solidarity** — whether the wider ecosystem rewards cooperation, exit, and public accountability over lock-in.
6. **Symbiosis** — whether the system stays bounded, local, and *sunset-ready* instead of hardening into permanent rule.

## Alignment as a public process

The load-bearing idea is that alignment is not solved once and installed. It is **maintained in public** — an accountable civic procedure the authors call *alignment-by-process*. This complements the safety work done inside frontier models; it does not replace it. Where a frontier lab asks "how do we build one aligned system," the 6-Pack asks how to keep answerability in the rooms where deployed systems already act: care homes, classrooms, councils, [deliberation platforms](/concepts/ai-and-deliberation/). It is the same instinct behind Audrey Tang's [plurality](/concepts/plurality/) — spread capability sideways, steer it with communities — applied to the governance of AI itself.

The framework draws openly on Joan Tronto's ethics of care, deliberative democracy, and Plurality. Its authors are blunt about the risk of empty relabelling:

:::tip[The test that keeps it honest]
If a principle does not change *who can object, who must answer, what gets logged, or what happens after a failure*, it is decoration.
:::

## Invite it, hold it accountable, retire it

In *Humane Radicalism*, Tang develops the same approach through three questions a community can ask before using AI:

- **Who invited it?** Members choose the question and write an amendable charter for the tool. Using an app does not by itself authorise its owner to decide for a community.
- **Who answers for it?** A named human checks and signs the machine's output, and can be asked to correct it. Tang compares this to a newspaper's byline, editor and corrections page. The record should show how dissent changed a decision, alongside the testimony and notes behind any summary; see [orphan reasons](/concepts/orphan-reasons/).
- **Who can retire it?** Name someone within the community who can end its use. Plan and budget for that hand-back at the start, leaving records and tools in forms the community can use independently. Judge success partly by what people can do together after the machine leaves.

Tang also asks for more than consent and exit: communities should be able to alter a local version, propose useful changes to the wider system, retune it and retire it. Her proposal for connecting these communities combines a shared floor of rights, portable memberships and records, and ways to see other communities' reasons. A local charter cannot lower that rights floor. This is a design argument, rather than evidence that every civic-AI deployment already meets these conditions.

In her preceding essay, *Dismantling MaxOS for Democracy*, she describes the accountable route from participation to action: visible inputs, checkable synthesis, named human judgment, a way to contest it and a receiver with authority to act. A summary alone does not complete that route. In a comment, Jan Bunge adds that the record of influence and responsibility must survive a change of technology.

## Bound the deployment, not just the model

In *AI and democracy: the right to resist optimization*, Tang makes an important distinction: **boundedness is a property of deployment, not of capability**. A general-purpose foundation model can support a narrow, democratically defined task. The important questions are who sets that task, where its mandate ends and how the people affected can challenge its use.

She calls for a named custodian, inspectable records, an appeal route and the ability to pause, override or retire the system. A translation aid should not quietly acquire a mandate to persuade people politically. Her alternative to a universal assistant is a federation of interoperable systems with local accountability. These are proposed governance conditions, not a claim that using a smaller model or hosting it locally supplies them automatically.

## Where it comes from

The 6-Pack grew out of real deployments on "unusually prepared soil" — places with civic hackers, responsive institutions, and strong community life. Its case studies include Taiwan's [Alignment Assemblies](/run-reports/taiwan-alignment-assemblies/) (which used [Polis](/toolkit/decide-together/)-style sensemaking to respond to a wave of AI-enabled scam ads), Team Mirai in Japan, [Engaged California](/run-reports/engaged-california/), the Tibetan-language Monlam AI, and the ROOST federated safety network. The authors are careful that each is proof of a mechanism, not a plug-and-play template: in a new setting you start again by asking who is missing, who has standing, and who can stop the system.

Civic AI in this sense is less about smarter chatbots than stronger self-government: institutions that show their work, repair harm in public, and carry civic memory across generations. It is a close cousin of [conversation networks](/concepts/conversation-networks/) and the wider argument that AI is a [social technology](/concepts/civic-ai/) to be governed, not an autonomous mind to be contained.
