---
title: Growing the civic-tech field
description: Why Europe's participation tools stay small and stuck — weak demand, a fragile fragmented supply, and procurement that shuts out the little builders — and the "civic tech hub" proposed to fix it.
---

There are thousands of civic-technology tools, and yet the field stays stuck: most tools stay small, many barely survive, and the same barriers recur everywhere. The problem is usually not the technology but the **ecosystem** around it. A 2026 report by [mySociety](/ecosystem/overview/) for [Sitra](/ecosystem/overview/), the Finnish innovation fund — an input to the European Commission's Democracy Shield — diagnoses why Europe's "participation tech" underperforms and proposes a lightweight **European civic tech hub** to help it grow up. It draws on interviews with 19 providers across 12 countries.

## Three things holding it back

- **Weak demand.** Public authorities don't yet treat participation as a credible governance tool. Without officials who want it, even excellent tools get "readily sidelined."
- **A fragile, fragmented supply.** Most providers are small and self-funded, in a European market estimated to be worth under €100 million, and split over whether to be open-source (good for digital sovereignty, but hard to fund) or proprietary (faster, but risking vendor lock-in). They compete for scarce resources while wanting to collaborate.
- **Procurement that excludes the small.** The certification, paperwork, and compliance costs of selling to government fall hardest on exactly the small builders the field depends on — too small for the big contracts, too undercapitalised to grow past that. One provider called it, simply, "a structural trap."

:::caution[The bind the whole supply side is in]
"We're all trying to walk a fine line of being competitors but wanting to collaborate."
:::

## It's a leadership and power problem, too

The report's launch discussion added the view from inside government, and it sharpens the picture: the deepest barrier practitioners named was not ignorance but a **leadership gap** — senior civil servants, not only politicians, unwilling to champion participation — compounded by a perception that digital participation is uniquely risky and *uncontrollable* (associated with social-media trolling). As one participant put it bluntly, "it's a power problem. People in power don't want power to be decentralized." Officials also carry the procurement burden without the procurement expertise to manage it.

## What a hub would actually do

Crucially, the proposed hub is **not another platform** competing with the tools — it is connective infrastructure. The concrete suggestions are modest and practical: a lightweight "front door" that signposts the resources that already exist (the [Civic Tech Field Guide](/ecosystem/overview/), [People Powered](/ecosystem/overview/), ACTE) rather than duplicating them; authority-facing case studies that show officials *how* participation delivered, not just that it did; practical interoperability standards; guidance on routes to market beyond public procurement (schools, charities, universities); sandboxes and demo instances so an official can try a tool without a sales call; and, in the long run, procurement reform. The through-line is closing the loop between what citizens contribute and a visible change they can point to.

## Where it sits

This is the field-level cousin of [why civic tech projects fail](/concepts/why-civic-tech-fails/) (which is about individual projects) and of [who builds civic AI](/concepts/who-builds-civic-ai/) (the funding and incumbency forces that shape what gets built). The hub itself is the practical face of [interoperability and standards](/concepts/interoperability-and-standards/) — and it is striking that the *same* "European Civic Tech Hub" surfaces in a peer-reviewed [analysis of civic-tech interoperability](/concepts/interoperability-and-standards/) and in the EU's own [four futures for democracy](/concepts/four-futures-for-ai-and-democracy/). Three independent inputs converging on one idea is a sign the field's connective tissue is finally being taken seriously.
