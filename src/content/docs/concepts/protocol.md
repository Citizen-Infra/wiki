title: Protocol
description: A neglected but ancient form of human organization — from indigenous traditions to internet standards — that may matter more now than ever.
---

We tend to think of a "protocol" as something technical and dull — an internet standard, a piece of plumbing. Nathan Schneider argues it's much bigger than that: **protocol is one of the central, and most overlooked, forms of human organization**, and relearning how to use it matters more now than ever.

## More than internet plumbing

The word *protocol* carries surprisingly deep meanings across very different worlds:

- In many **indigenous** contexts, protocol names sovereign practices, rituals, and traditions — ways of knowing who you are even under colonisation — and also the **membrane between a community and the state** (in parts of Latin America and Canada, "protocol" is the word for the proper relationship between governments and indigenous communities).
- There are **climate** protocols, **internet** protocols, and now protocols being written as **guardrails for AI**.

What links them is that a protocol coordinates behaviour *without* a central ruler — it's a shared, repeatable pattern that lets independent parties act together. That makes it a powerful, under-appreciated alternative to top-down control, for organising both human communities and our relationship with our environment.

## Why it matters

For citizen infrastructure, protocols are a reminder that governing together doesn't always mean building an institution or an app. Sometimes it means agreeing on a *protocol* — a shared way of relating that many different groups can adopt and adapt. It's a complement to the more platform-centric idea of [modular politics](/concepts/modular-politics/).

## Patterns of interaction, not just control

Schneider defines a protocol broadly as a **pattern of interaction** — wide enough to hold internet standards, religious rituals, and a region's dialect all at once, so the useful work is in the *distinctions* you draw within. He pushes back on an influential older view (Alexander Galloway's 2004 *Protocol*) that cast protocol mainly as a hidden form of **control**: protocols are also **sites of creativity**. Two ideas from that work travel well:

- **Vernacular protocols** — not only the rule-sets elites design and impose from the top down, but the ones communities *outside* institutional power build all the time (a local dialect is itself a vernacular protocol). Trans and other marginalised communities, for instance, develop rich protocols for care and survival.
- **Protocol sovereignty** — a protocol controlled by no single entity (like the internet's TCP/IP) can take on a power of its own. Getting there means entangling many stakeholders, which is also what creates the risk of **capture** by corporate or state power.

Schneider also argues protocols were the *dominant* form of human organisation long before the nation-state — which itself he reads as just one "protocol of stateness," historically an aberration — and that, since no single state can govern the climate or the internet, protocols are becoming central again.

## An open process still needs care

Protocols do not come only from elegant rules. Lisa Dusseault's [oral history of interoperability standards](https://protocol.ecologies.info/interviews/dusseault-interoperability/) describes the relationships, institutional interests and patient implementation work behind them. She warns that an open mailing list can still exclude people if hostile encounters drive them away. The point is not that openness fails, but that **access on paper and the ability to keep participating are different things**. See [standards, participation and implementation](/concepts/interoperability-and-standards/#standards-need-people-and-implementations).

## Relationships are not just a technical analogy

Rick Williams's [oral history](https://protocol.ecologies.info/interviews/williams-restorative_justice/) connects agreements with continuing responsibilities between people and with land. His account of restoration concerns cultural continuity, resources and participation in stewardship. It is a particular person's account grounded in his Oglala Lakota and Cheyenne identity, not a universal Indigenous protocol to copy into an organisation or an app. See [restoration, relationships and resources](/concepts/relational-property/#restoration-involves-relationships-and-resources).

## Leaving and having a say are different

Bryan Newbold's protocol-engineering oral history separates **credible exit** from decentralisation and democratic governance. A person may be able to change providers without having a vote over the shared rules. Whether that choice is practical also depends on independent providers, resources and usable migration tools. See [credible exit and its limits](/concepts/decentralized-landscape/#credible-exit-is-not-the-same-as-governance).

## Agree what happens when people cannot agree

Michael Zargham's [ultimate-frisbee oral history](https://protocol.ecologies.info/interviews/zargham-ultimate_frisbee/) offers a bounded example of self-governance. He describes learning not just a rulebook, but how to make a call, hear a disagreement and keep playing. He also describes teaching newcomers, volunteer leadership and the work of handing roles on.

The **[WFDF Rules of Ultimate 2025–2028](https://rules.wfdf.sport/)** make responsibilities explicit: players administer the rules, teams teach them, and experienced players help novices. Rule 1.12 supplies a fallback when discussion cannot establish what happened: return the disc to the last undisputed thrower. Shared norms do not remove the need for a defined procedure when interpretations differ.

This is an example to think with, not a rule to transplant into every community. Sporting contexts have agreed boundaries, and competition formats vary. Their procedures do not establish how to protect rights, address abuse or resolve unequal power in civic life; see [Working with conflict](/concepts/working-with-conflict/).
