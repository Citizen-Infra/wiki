---
title: AI for participation
description: How AI is actually showing up in participatory democracy tools — translation, summarising, sensemaking — and where the line is.
---

When people talk about AI for democracy, the most concrete place it's already landing is **participation tools** — the platforms that let residents deliberate, propose, and decide together (think participatory budgeting, or a city consultation). A 2025 study of around 30 participation platforms already using AI, by People Powered with the Civic Tech Field Guide, found a couple of dozen distinct features. A few patterns stand out.

## Nine jobs AI can do

The [OECD and Bertelsmann Stiftung's 2026 report](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/artificial-intelligence-and-the-future-of-citizen-participation_a1ee2e0a-en.html) maps **50 uses across 22 countries** into nine types of application. These are different jobs within a participation process: organisers might use one tool to transcribe a meeting and another to analyse the contributions. The report distinguishes help for participants from work inside government, such as processing submissions or configuring a platform.

| Application | What it helps people do |
|---|---|
| Information development | Prepare background material or draft a contribution. |
| Sense-making | Group contributions, find themes and identify unusual views; see [AI sensemaking](/concepts/ai-sensemaking/). |
| Translation | Read and contribute in different languages. |
| Transcription | Turn speech into text, or make written information available as speech. |
| Virtual assistance | Ask questions and find information about how to participate. |
| Moderation | Detect spam, abusive content and attempts to flood a consultation. |
| Facilitation | Support live discussion and identify shared positions. |
| Simulation | Explore policy options through scenarios and visualisations. |
| Participation architecture | Structure online spaces for constructive exchange, as with [Polis](/toolkit/decide-together/). |

Source: the report's [typology and examples](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/artificial-intelligence-and-the-future-of-citizen-participation_a1ee2e0a-en/full-report/opportunities-for-ai-tools-to-improve-and-enhance-citizen-participation-in-policymaking_1793beb2.html). The categories describe intended uses, rather than guarantees that a tool works well. The report identifies [**32 of its 50 cases** as pilots or one-off initiatives](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/artificial-intelligence-and-the-future-of-citizen-participation_a1ee2e0a-en/full-report/mapping-the-risks-and-challenges-of-using-ai-for-citizen-participation_f3ffbba1.html); this is a map of selected practice, not an estimate of worldwide adoption or a comparative test of effectiveness.

## What AI is doing well

- **Translation.** Running a participatory process in the dozens of languages spoken in a place like New York is hard and expensive. AI translation — improving even for "low-resource" languages — can get many more people to a usable level of understanding. It's not just cost-saving; it's reach.
- **Summarising and classifying.** If a city process generates 40,000 pages of resident feedback, no one can read it all. Natural-language processing can summarise debates, sort comments into themes, and synthesise long conversations. (This predates the generative-AI boom, which is why it's the most common feature so far.)
- **Sensemaking.** Helping people understand *what came out* of a process — and, crucially, where their own contribution landed. Done well, this addresses a deep weakness of representative systems: that you give up some immediate say for a collective outcome, and rarely get to see what it bought.
- **Support at the individual level.** Assistants that help a participant flesh out an idea so others can understand it.

## Where to be careful

AI here should augment a process, not run it. Sentiment estimation still isn't fully accurate, summaries can miss whole sections, and sensitive engagements need human review. The aim is to let institutions open up *more often* by lowering the cost — not to take people out of the loop. The sharpest caution is its own topic: see [synthetic participation](/concepts/synthetic-participation/).

The OECD identifies specific ways that assistance can go wrong: a summary can erase a minority view, automated moderation can silence a position, and weak performance in a language can cause its speakers' contributions to be undervalued. Its [risk analysis](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/artificial-intelligence-and-the-future-of-citizen-participation_a1ee2e0a-en/full-report/mapping-the-risks-and-challenges-of-using-ai-for-citizen-participation_f3ffbba1.html) also warns that predicting citizens' opinions must not replace involving them.

For organisers, the report's [practical guidance](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/artificial-intelligence-and-the-future-of-citizen-participation_a1ee2e0a-en/full-report/what-can-governments-do-the-road-to-fit-for-purpose-ai-tools-for-citizen-participation_06b838ee.html) calls for disclosing where AI is used, protecting participants' data, providing ways to challenge problems, and retaining in-person or low-tech routes. Efficiency gains can help fund those alternatives. Human judgment still matters when checking what an AI summary includes and whose contribution it may have missed.
