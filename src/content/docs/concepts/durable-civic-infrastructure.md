---
title: Durable civic infrastructure
description: A citizens' assembly is an event. What a community actually needs is the standing capacity to keep doing democracy — built locally, kept small, and made to last between the headline moments.
---

It is easy to be dazzled by the set-piece: a [citizens' assembly](/concepts/citizens-assembly/) convenes, deliberates, hands over its recommendations, and disbands. **Durable civic infrastructure** is the argument that the event is the least interesting part. The phrase belongs to Richard Young, director of the Lexington, Kentucky non-profit [CivicLex](/ecosystem/overview/), whose work running the [Lexington Civic Assembly](/run-reports/lexington-civic-assembly/) led him to a deliberately unglamorous conclusion: what a community needs is not more one-off assemblies but the *standing capacity* to keep doing democracy, the local journalism, civic education, public-input habits and relationships that persist between the headline moments.

## Scale down, not up

The instinct in democratic innovation is to scale *up*: bigger assemblies, national platforms, a model that spreads everywhere. Young's counter-instinct is to **scale down, not up**, to invest in dense, place-based civic capacity in one community rather than thin coverage everywhere. The pay-off is durability: infrastructure that is woven into a specific place, with people who know each other and stick around, survives the change of administration that kills a free-floating process. It is the same insight behind [locality-to-locality spreading](/concepts/locality-to-locality-spreading/) (tools spread by being remixed in a new place, not franchised from the centre) and the slow work of [institutionalising deliberation](/concepts/institutionalising-deliberation/).

## An assembly is a tool, not a cure

The corollary is a useful humility: a citizens' assembly is **a tool, not a cure-all**. It is very good at a particular job, giving a representative cross-section of residents the time and information to make a hard collective judgement, and useless at the jobs around it: building trust, keeping people informed week to week, holding officials to account between assemblies, sustaining the relationships that make any of it stick. Treating the assembly as the whole of civic life is how it ends up [rehearsing its own inferiority](/concepts/institutionalising-deliberation/) or becoming a [one-off artifact with no people behind it](/concepts/cargo-cult-democracy/). Treating it as *one instrument in a standing civic toolkit*, alongside the durable infrastructure, is what lets it matter. This is why the organisations that act as [scaling catalysts](/concepts/scaling-catalysts/) increasingly measure themselves by the lasting capacity they leave behind, not the number of assemblies they run.

## Communities and public services

Neighbourhood capacity also matters in how public services work with residents. Cormac Russell and Lisa McNally's neighbourhood-health provocation asks whether institutions support residents' relationships, associations and plans, with resources and decision power to match. [Community power in health](/concepts/community-power-in-health/) links their practical questions for residents, councillors, system leaders and practitioners. This is an institutional proposal, not proof that a particular programme improves health outcomes.

## Continuity does not require every organisation to last forever

Camille Acey distinguishes a movement's purpose from the lifespan of any one organisation. In her [oral history of organisational closures](https://protocol.ecologies.info/interviews/acey-organizational_closures/), she describes planning for endings at the beginning and revisiting whether work should continue. A pause, a handover and a closure are different possibilities; her role is not to decide for a group that it must close.

Her emphasis has shifted from archiving alone towards relationships as well as practical work: communicating with affected people, recognising contributions, addressing remaining responsibilities and finding appropriate stewards for useful resources. The [coordination toolkit](/toolkit/coordinate-a-community/#plan-handovers-pauses-and-endings) links her public reflection and final-website resources. These are practice-based suggestions, not evidence that a checklist guarantees a painless or legally complete closure.

For civic infrastructure, the question is what capacity remains available to people—not whether every project survives indefinitely. A useful archive still needs a steward and resources; a referral still needs a receiving organisation able to help. Ending deliberately can be a stewardship decision, rather than simply abandoning people or keeping an exhausted organisation going at any cost.

## Teach the practices, not just the rules

In his [ultimate-frisbee oral history](https://protocol.ecologies.info/interviews/zargham-ultimate_frisbee/), Michael Zargham describes newcomers learning from experienced players and student teams passing responsibilities between cohorts. His later volunteer roles involved disputes and the practical work of keeping leagues going, not simply designing a structure once. The lesson he draws is about sustained attention to norms and practices. It is a participant's account, not an evaluation showing that a sport's model will work for every civic organisation. See [procedures for disagreement](/concepts/protocol/#agree-what-happens-when-people-cannot-agree).

## People need time as well as an invitation

In his [union-negotiating oral history](https://protocol.ecologies.info/interviews/reddy-union_negotiating/), Vishal Reddy argues that shorter working time could make more room for family, community and other parts of life. His account also distinguishes changing a policy on paper from protecting the resulting time through expectations, communication and ongoing collective attention.

For civic organisers, the relevant question is whether people have the time and resources to participate, not simply whether a meeting is open to them. Reddy's argument is not evidence that a shorter workweek necessarily increases civic participation or works identically in every workplace. [WorkFour](/ecosystem/overview/) campaigns for a four-day, thirty-two-hour week with full pay and benefits; that aim is not a statement of universal legal entitlement.

## Steward public software beyond the prototype

In Martijn de Waal's [DWeb Camp account](https://martijndewaal.nl/a/3mrx3d67o5h23-root-systems-towards-a-decentralized-internet-at-dweb-camp), Ben Cerveny proposes **Public Product Organisations** as vehicles for ongoing stewardship of shared public software. The aim is to maintain and develop a common product with participating institutions, while allowing adaptation to local needs, rather than treating a promising prototype as the end of the work.

The [official session abstract](https://talx.dod.ngo/dwebcamp-2026/talk/ATLKXZ/) identifies this as a policy proposal. It raises concrete questions for a community relying on public software: who will maintain it, who pays after initial funding ends, and how do users and participating institutions influence its direction? The [Foundation for Public Code](/ecosystem/overview/) works on these stewardship questions; the proposal is not, by itself, proof of an enacted legal category or a particular product's long-term viability.
