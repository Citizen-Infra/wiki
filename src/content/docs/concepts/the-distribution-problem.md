---
title: The distribution problem
description: The neglected half of civic tech — a brilliant tool nobody uses is worthless. Reaching people means going where they already are (social apps, messaging, radio, trusted messengers), not waiting on a website.
---

Civic technology has a habit of pouring its effort into building the tool and almost none into getting it used. A team ships a genuinely useful thing — a way to contact your representative, track a budget, or join a consultation — puts it on a website, and waits. Almost no one comes. **Reaching an audience is a second product**, roughly as hard as the first, and it is the half the field most often skips. Practitioners who take it seriously treat *distribution* as a discipline in its own right.

## Go where people already are

The core mistake is expecting citizens to come to a civic website. They won't: attention has moved to social platforms, messaging apps, and, in much of the world, radio. So the work is to meet people in those channels rather than publish into a void. The website becomes the archive and the press-of-record; **social media is the discovery layer** — short video, quizzes, a WhatsApp chatbot that answers "who is my representative?" without anyone having to visit a new site at all.

## Trust the messenger, not the institution

Reach also depends on *who* carries the message. Content from an official account speaking official language travels badly; the same information from a creator the audience already trusts travels far. That means partnering with influencers and community figures — and giving them creative freedom rather than a script, because the trust is theirs, not yours.

:::tip[The line that names the failure]
"We must reach them where they are, otherwise you become a traditional parliamentary org, speaking to itself, writing shiny reports with very little impact."
:::

## What it looks like in practice

A [mySociety](/ecosystem/overview/)-hosted session gathered three vivid cases, all from outside the usual Global-North frame:

- **Georgia** — a Gen-Z pro-democracy campaign worked with 17 influencers across 145 videos and, by its own account, reached roughly 9 million views in a country of about 4 million, at engagement rates well above a marketing baseline.
- **Nigeria** — **Enough is Enough**'s *Shine Your Eye* / *Know Your Rep* used a WhatsApp chatbot so citizens could look up and email their representative from the app they already use; in one case a constituent's message prompted an MP to investigate and fix a stalled local project.
- **Uganda** — **Parliament Watch** built distribution around the channels people actually use: radio reaches over half the population, so it runs a media network of dozens of stations and journalists, with short social video (TikTok and WhatsApp each carrying a large share of traffic) funnelling people toward the deeper trackers.

## The metric that matters

The trap is to measure *reach* — raw views — and feel successful. What the practitioners watch instead is **engagement**: are people actually acting, sharing, coming back? And there is a tipping point worth knowing: reach something like 40–50% of a target audience and word-of-mouth starts doing the spreading for you.

## Where it sits

Distribution is the flip side of [why civic tech projects fail](/concepts/why-civic-tech-fails/): many good tools die not from bad code but from never being found. It is the same instinct as the [organizer kit](/concepts/organizer-kit/) (meet the under-resourced organizer where they already are), and it leans on the human networks behind [relational tech](/concepts/relational-tech/) and [locality-to-locality spreading](/concepts/locality-to-locality-spreading/): in the end, trusted people carry tools further than any launch.
