---
title: Open networks
description: Connecting buyers and providers across apps—and asking who controls participation, fees, data and the shared rules.
---

Must a local trader, driver or customer join the same platform to find one another and do business? **Open networks** offer a different arrangement: participating services use a shared protocol to exchange information and coordinate transactions, while people can use different compatible apps.

In his Protocol Oral History interview, **Sujith Nair** describes this as distributing the ability to solve problems rather than building one platform to solve everything. His example is **Beckn**, a protocol whose published core specification covers discovery, ordering, fulfilment and post-fulfilment interactions between providers and consumers. It is a common technical approach, not a single marketplace that everyone signs up to.

## Separate the protocol from the network

Three layers need different questions:

- **The protocol:** What messages can services exchange, and who can change that specification?
- **The network:** Which organisations can participate, under what rules, and who handles disputes or sanctions?
- **The apps and services:** What do people actually experience—prices, fees, accessibility, data handling and support?

Beckn's published governance and network-policy documents distinguish specification maintenance from rules for particular networks. The reviewed documents include dated drafts; they do not establish every network's current procedures. A common message format does not, by itself, give participants control over a marketplace or make its terms fair.

## Questions for a community considering a network

1. **Can people enter?** Who approves providers and apps, what does joining cost, and who is excluded in practice?
2. **Who sets the terms?** Who controls prices, fees, visibility and access to customers? Can workers or small providers challenge changes?
3. **Who answers when something goes wrong?** Where can people dispute a transaction, appeal a suspension or seek a remedy?
4. **What happens to data and consent?** Which participants receive personal information, for what purpose, and with what safeguards?
5. **Can people leave—and have a say?** Can they change apps or providers without losing essential relationships or records? Do they also have a voice in the rules?

These are questions to investigate, not a certification checklist. As with [credible exit](/concepts/decentralized-landscape/#credible-exit-is-not-the-same-as-governance), an option in the architecture may be difficult to exercise without usable services, resources and support.

## Open does not mean ungoverned

Nair argues that governance should evolve alongside adoption. He also describes forking as a fallback when governance breaks down, while warning that divergent versions can lose interoperability. These are design judgments, not evidence that adoption prevents capture or that participants should wait for protection until a network grows.

Nor should “open” be read as one unrestricted licence. The reviewed Beckn core repository carries **CC BY-NC-SA 4.0**, while its historical network-policy draft declares **CC-BY-ND**. Check the terms of the particular specification, implementation and material you plan to reuse; they need not be the same.

## What would demonstrate the promise?

Look for evidence about real participation, terms, bargaining power, privacy, remedies and operational alternatives—not just transaction volumes or an open repository. Nair's interview offers a builder's account and ambitions; this guide does not establish improved incomes, financial inclusion or immunity from government or corporate control.

Open networks connect the practical work of [interoperability](/concepts/interoperability-and-standards/) with [modular governance](/concepts/modular-politics/): shared technical components still need institutions that people can understand, influence and hold accountable.
