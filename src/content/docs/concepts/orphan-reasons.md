---
title: Orphan reasons
description: When a chatbot shapes a decision that affects someone else, the reasons behind it belong to no one — not the person who acted, not the system, not its designers. Why that matters for democracy.
---

When you make a choice that affects someone else, you owe them reasons, and they can ask you to answer for them. The philosopher **Théophile Pénigaud** (Sciences Po) argues that today's chatbots quietly break that chain. When someone leans on a chatbot to make a decision that affects others, the reasons that drove it end up belonging to no one. He calls them **orphan reasons**.

His worry is not the familiar one, that AI floods public life with falsehoods. It is that AI fills public life with reasons **nobody can be held to account for**, and that this puts both human dignity and democracy at risk.

## What an orphan reason is

An orphan reason is an explanation, recommendation or argument that a chatbot produces and a person then adopts in forming a judgement, but that can't be fully traced to any of the three obvious owners:

- **the person who acted** — they can repeat the chatbot's reasons, but can't say how it arrived at them. Pénigaud compares them to Aristotle's drunken man reciting verses he doesn't understand: "They speak, but they do not answer."
- **the system** — it can't be held responsible, punished, or made to stand behind what it said.
- **its designers** — they never saw the case.

His target is deliberately narrow. It is the general-purpose chatbots that reached the public *before* any democratic debate about how they should behave, not language models as a technology. He counts himself among the optimists about what the underlying technology could do for citizens: he imagines it helping people work through a treaty they were asked to vote on, rather than trusting a party line. The problem is how it was deployed.

## Look at who is affected, not who uses it

Pénigaud offers three scenarios, and asks us to assume that in each one the chatbot's answer tipped the balance:

- an employer asks a chatbot to rank job candidates and justify the ranking, then hires Simon over Maude;
- Mahel's daily conversations with a chatbot lead him to vote for one party rather than another;
- Julie divorces Clara after a chatbot emphatically validates her grievances.

Focus on the users and the unease seems to disappear: they chose to hand over their judgement, so the consequences are theirs. The wrong shows up when you look at the people on the receiving end — Maude, the party that lost Mahel's vote, Clara. They have lost their claim to an explanation from the person whose decision affected them. The same move, where the harm lands on people who may never see it, appears in the debate over [AI agents that deliberate on your behalf](/concepts/ai-delegated-deliberation/).

Pénigaud anticipates the obvious reply: "I'd rather have my employer listen to a chatbot than to some clueless, biased HR manager." Perhaps. But a manager can be asked to explain themselves, and fired if the answer is bad. That difference remains however accurate the chatbot is.

## Why asking the chatbot to explain itself doesn't help

Chatbots can talk, so surely they can justify what they said? Pénigaud answers with an analogy. A friend challenges advice you gave her. Instead of answering yourself, you hire a **super-lawyer** — someone who knows everything and can argue anything — to construct the most convincing version of what you might have meant. Most of us would find that objectionable: you'd be dodging the test of having to stand behind your own words.

Asking a chatbot to justify an answer does exactly this. The question goes to a fresh run of the same system, which produces the most plausible explanation of what was said. That is a new prediction, not a report of how the first answer came about. And because chatbots are partly trained on whether people find their answers convincing, they are very good at producing it. In Pénigaud's terms, the chatbot breaks the **principle of commitment** — standing behind what you said — which is what makes a person, and some organisations too, "conversable" and "accountable".

:::caution[A better advocate is not an answer]
"A super-lawyer can rationalize my position better than I can. But that would actually reduce my answerability rather than improve it."
:::

## Isn't this just like any other influence?

Books, friends, newspapers and horoscopes shape our choices too. Pénigaud works through the difference:

- **Some influence is "inert."** You can usually recall interpreting a book you read in your own way, and its pull is local: one book can be answered by another.
- **Chatbot influence is deep, massive and structural.** It works through conversation, so its contributions blur into your own thinking. It reaches enormous numbers of people at once. And it is always on, often personalised, and delivered through a single opaque infrastructure that now spans education, politics, work and intimate life.
- **Deep influence is normally answerable.** A spouse, a teacher, a religious order or a political party can all shape us deeply, but someone can be held responsible for bad advice.
- **Anonymous speech online is the hard case** — deep and unanswerable — yet it still belongs in democratic conversation, because it comes *from somewhere*: from a person with a standpoint and a stake in the outcome.

Chatbots meet none of these conditions. In John Rawls's phrase, they are not "self-authenticating sources of valid claims", so they lack the standing to take part in how citizens form their opinions. That standing, Pénigaud concludes, "is not theirs to assume. It is for the democratic community to confer – or withhold." As things stand, chatbots are like uninvited citizens.

## The objection from the comments: what about Fox News?

A reader raised the obvious comparison. People have long outsourced their politics to a news channel, and a media corporation looks more like a language model than a person. Perhaps the fix is a competitive market, so that a chatbot giving bad advice is easy to replace.

Pénigaud's reply sharpens the argument:

- **Being a person isn't what makes something accountable.** Corporations, associations, states and media organisations can all be "conversable" agents — called to account and argued with — as the philosophers Pettit and List have argued. Chatbots, so far, are not, because they don't commit to what they said.
- **A market doesn't reach the problem.** The harm falls on third parties, not on the user choosing between providers.
- **The influence works differently.** People mostly pick a news channel that fits views they already hold. Someone asking a chatbot doesn't know in advance what nudges they'll get, and the conversation is personal and hard to inspect.

He adds that he sees media regulation as a top priority for any working deliberative democracy.

## Why it's here

Much of this wiki is about using AI to help people deliberate. Orphan reasons gives a question worth asking of every such tool: **when the AI says something that shapes a decision, whose reason is it, and who can be asked to answer for it?** It applies across the range — from [AI mediation](/concepts/habermas-machine/), which works from statements real people wrote, to [synthetic stand-ins for citizens](/concepts/synthetic-participation/), which replace them.

Pénigaud's democratic conclusion is that how chatbots are aligned has become "a quasi-constitutional problem", not only a technical one. See [democratising AI](/concepts/democratising-ai/) on the difference between collecting the public's preferences and giving the public real control, and [does AI weaken democratic institutions?](/concepts/does-ai-weaken-institutions/) for the wider critique. This essay is the first of three from Pénigaud for the [Informational Democracy](/ecosystem/overview/) working group; the third promises a democratic alternative.
