---
title: Coordinate a group or community
description: Tools that help a community organise itself — set its own rules, roles, and ways of making decisions.
---

Before a group can act together, it has to organise itself: agree on who decides what, how, and by what rules. Most online communities never get this far — they default to one all-powerful admin (what Nathan Schneider calls [implicit feudalism](/concepts/implicit-feudalism/)). These tools help a group do better, by designing its own governance deliberately.

- **[CommunityRule](https://communityrule.info/)** (Media Economies Design Lab) — a simple drag-and-drop web app for designing, editing, and sharing your community's own governance: its norms for decision-making, stewardship, and culture. It ships with templates, so a group can start from a known pattern rather than a blank page — or the default of handing one person all the power.
- **[Governable Spaces MCP](https://bookpower.org/governable-spaces/)** — a public, book-grounded assistant that can diagnose implicit feudalism, find precedents, and compare governance forms such as sortition, federated subsidiarity, and plural voting.

## Document community governance

- **[Governance Cards — research prototype](https://blog.citp.princeton.edu/2026/09/18/making-community-governance-legible-a-semester-with-bonfire/)** — Princeton's CROW project and Bonfire explore concise descriptions of a community's infrastructure, membership and rules. The published examples can help a group think about what to document and what should remain private. As of 21 October 2026, [crowcards.org](https://crowcards.org/) still says “Coming soon”; it is not a released card-building app. The final schema and sharing controls remain work in progress. See [making governance legible](/concepts/modular-politics/#making-governance-legible).

## Work with public services

- **[Community power in health](/concepts/community-power-in-health/)** — a resident-facing checklist and links to four question guides from Cormac Russell and Lisa McNally, hosted by New Local. Use them to discuss priorities, decision rights, funding, inclusion and reporting back with local health and public-service leaders. The guides come from a UK policy context and are discussion resources, not clinical advice or a validated programme score.

## Set expectations and a way to address harm

**[Contributor Covenant](https://www.contributor-covenant.org/)** is a code-of-conduct resource for digital communities, created by Coraline Ada Ehmke and stewarded by the Organization for Ethical Source. Its current [3.0 text](https://www.contributor-covenant.org/version/3/0/) covers expected behaviour, reporting and responses to harm. Use the [adaptation guide](https://www.contributor-covenant.org/adopt) to review it for your community; the static text still contains reporting placeholders that you must replace.

Agree who receives reports, who can make enforcement decisions, what confidentiality means and how decisions can be questioned. The suggested consequence ladder is guidance, not an automatic punishment algorithm. The text also distinguishes ordinary conflict from a code violation. A policy on a website is not proof that people can report safely or receive a fair response; see [a covenant and its working process](/concepts/modular-politics/#a-covenant-needs-a-working-process).

The source text uses **CC BY-SA 4.0**. Follow its reuse conditions if adapting it; this guide links the material rather than republishing a community's completed policy. No report, policy submission or download workflow was tested here.

## Consider who governs your host

**[CoSocial](https://cosocial.ca/about)** is a cooperative-run social-media service for Canada using Mastodon. Its public description says members vote at meetings, elect directors and join working groups. Its [membership page](https://join.cosocial.ca/sign-up/) sets individual adult/Canadian eligibility and Canada-based organisation requirements, with membership fees and an invitation process. Registration and service delivery were not tested for this guide.

It is an example to examine, not a claim that every federated server is member-run. When choosing a host, review who appoints administrators, makes moderation rules, hears appeals and pays ongoing costs. See [federation versus member control](/concepts/decentralized-landscape/#federation-and-member-control-are-different).

## Prepare for difficult conversations

- **[Working with conflict](/concepts/working-with-conflict/)** — questions about purpose, roles, power, consent and confidentiality, grounded in Bernard Mayer's account of conflict engagement and professional mediation guidance. A starting point for choosing appropriate support, not a mediation qualification or a guarantee that dialogue is suitable or safe.

## Plan handovers, pauses and endings

**[The Wind Down](https://www.wind-down.org/resources/)**, run by Camille E. Acey, offers resources for mission-driven groups considering or carrying out a closure. Its public [self-assessment](https://wind-down.org/resources/the-self-assessment/) combines reflection with practical questions about people, responsibilities and useful resources. It is non-exhaustive, not a score that tells you whether to close.

Its [final-website guide](https://www.wind-down.org/2024/10/22/towards-your-tombstone-site/) covers clear closure messages, signposts to other support, useful archives and a plan for the website's future. Check that a suggested successor has capacity before directing people there. Decide what can safely be shared rather than making all organisational records public.

Start the conversation before an emergency: who can authorise a pause or ending, who needs to hear, what commitments remain, and what could pass to another steward? These are planning resources, not legal dissolution, employment, financial or therapeutic advice. See [continuity without permanence](/concepts/durable-civic-infrastructure/#continuity-does-not-require-every-organisation-to-last-forever).

## Build coordination skills

- **[Network Weaving Game](https://www.network-weaver-game.com/)** — a Fito Network reflection tool for people coordinating networks and communities. Its questions cover cultivating networks, collaborating systemically, learning together and embodying wellbeing, with follow-on resources for planning and practice. Use it to identify learning goals; the public pages do not establish a validated score of a network's effectiveness. See [learning across a network](/concepts/learning-across-a-network/).
