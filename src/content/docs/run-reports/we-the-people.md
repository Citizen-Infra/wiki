title: We the People (2025)
description: How a national AI-facilitated deliberation gathered 2,400 Americans across all 435 congressional districts to define freedom and equality — and found far more agreement than the country's politics suggests.
---

**TL;DR.** In September 2025, as the United States approached its 250th anniversary, [Jigsaw](/ecosystem/overview/) (Google) and the [Napolitan Institute](/ecosystem/overview/) ran a national online deliberation called **We the People**. Over 2,400 people — at least one from every one of the 435 congressional districts — wrote, in their own words, what *freedom* and *equality* mean to them, then explored an AI-generated map of the whole conversation and rated draft consensus statements. AI [sensemaking](/concepts/ai-sensemaking/) distilled more than 1.6 million words into 26 summary statements, most of which drew agreement above 80% *across* partisan lines. The result is now archived in the American Philosophical Society.

## What happened

Instead of a poll's tick-boxes or a town hall's loudest voices, We the People asked open questions and let an AI facilitator follow up on each person's answer, the way a good interviewer would. That produced more than 1.6 million words — far too much for any team to read. Jigsaw's [Sensemaking](/concepts/ai-sensemaking/) tools clustered the responses, filed every statement, and wrote cited summaries, rendering the whole conversation as an interactive [opinion landscape](/concepts/how-we-picture-disagreement/) rather than a red-versus-blue bar chart. Participants then rated AI-drafted consensus statements, and the 26 that survived mostly cleared 80% agreement across party lines.

The self-reported experience was strikingly positive for a one-hour online exercise: **94%** felt their own views were accurately represented, **89%** felt it expanded the range of voices they heard, three in four (**75%**) came away understanding opposing views better, and **97%** said they would take part again. Those figures are participants' own reports, and the exercise was short and entirely online — but the direction is consistent with the paper's central claim: shown the full distribution of what people actually said, Americans found more common ground than the country's binary politics implies. Jigsaw open-sourced the conversation and visualisation code so others can reproduce the method.

## Why it matters

We the People is the national-scale cousin of Bowling Green's [What Could BG Be?](/run-reports/bowling-green-what-could-bg-be/): the same [AI sensemaking](/concepts/ai-sensemaking/) toolchain, aimed not at recruiting a representative few but at making sense of open-ended input from thousands. Two things make it notable. First, its scale — every congressional district, a genuinely national canvas. Second, its *form*: by presenting the result as an [opinion landscape](/concepts/how-we-picture-disagreement/) rather than two opposing bars, it doubles as evidence that how we picture disagreement changes how divided we feel. The honest caveats are the usual ones for this shape of work — self-reported satisfaction, a short online session, and summaries that still need people to interpret them — but as a demonstration that mass, AI-assisted deliberation can surface real cross-partisan agreement, it is one of the clearest yet.
