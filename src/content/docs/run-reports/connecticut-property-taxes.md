---
title: "Connecticut Citizens' Assembly on Property Taxes (2026)"
description: About 100 randomly selected Connecticut residents governed their own assembly, linked it to thousands of residents online, and agreed ten property-tax reforms. Legislative follow-up is pending.
---

Connecticut's property tax is the only major levy its 169 cities and towns can raise, and reform attempts have stalled for decades. In 2026 a randomly selected group of about 100 residents spent three months on the question and agreed **ten reform proposals**, from a millionaire's income tax and highway tolls to full state funding of special education. Its final report calls it the first large-scale, statewide citizens' assembly in the United States. It is unusually instructive on two fronts: the citizens **voted to govern the process themselves**, and the assembly tried to **connect the people in the room with the wider public**. Whether lawmakers act on the proposals is **still pending**.

## What happened

**Recruitment by lottery, with no volunteering.** Organisers mailed **222,060 invitations**: 100,000 to registered voters drawn at random, and 122,060 to every residential address in 26 randomly chosen ZIP codes, so that people not registered to vote could be reached too. Each letter carried a unique code; there was no open sign-up. Across two online surveys, **1,507 people** enrolled and committed to attend every session.

**Correcting a skewed pool.** Enrollees were far more Democratic (64% vs. 46% statewide) and whiter than Connecticut as a whole. So seats were allocated exactly by homeownership, children at home and region, and each candidate panel was then checked against state benchmarks on income, gender, race and ethnicity, urban/rural residence and party, and redrawn unless every measure came within 5.5 points. Of 50 million draws, 9,975 panels passed; 40 were numbered, and on 11 June 2026 the Connecticut Lottery's certified machine picked **Panel 29**.

**Getting people into the room is a separate problem.** Only 74 of the 110 drawn confirmed for the first session, so the empty seats were filled from a single random ordering of the other enrollees. The assembly that convened ended up more female, more Democratic and more suburban than the state, and short on independents, Hispanic and urban residents. The final report publishes this gap openly, session by session.

**Six sessions.** Members met from 11 July, mostly in person on the Yale campus in New Haven, with some sessions online. Each session mixed expert presentations, panels and small-group work, run by an independent facilitation team. Speakers included economists, municipal finance directors, mayors, first selectmen and legislative leaders from both parties. A Nor'easter on 26 September cancelled a session and pushed the final vote back to **10 October**.

**Citizens took the keys.** At their first working session on 11 July, members voted by secret ballot, **89 to 8 with 1 abstention**, to take procedural control from the interim expert governance committee. They ran the assembly through a seven-member executive committee drawn by lot and rotated every session, with a safeguard that any ten members could force a floor vote of the whole assembly. Members amended these rules as they went: adding a chair drawn from volunteers, lunchtime "office hours", and outgoing committee members sitting in as non-voting guides, partly to stop staff from capturing the process. They set their own adoption rule (two-thirds of voting members) and wrote the proposals themselves, with staff limited to note-taking and layout. They also elected eight media representatives to speak for the assembly. (See [letting citizens govern the assembly](/concepts/citizen-governance-of-assemblies/).)

**Linking to the wider public.** Two online platforms, [Make.org](/toolkit/decide-together/) and Ideas for Change, let any resident propose and vote on ideas: **over 4,700 residents contributed 700+ ideas and 80,000+ votes** from late June to the end of August. Members themselves voted to receive the results as a presentation plus a dashboard that used AI to cluster related ideas while keeping links back to each submission. Some members raised concerns about the AI. Offline, a series of "Great Ice Cream Assembly" socials at a Ben & Jerry's in downtown New Haven walked passers-by through the assembly and concrete propositions (such as scrapping the car tax) in short table conversations.

## Inputs → outputs

**In:** lottery recruitment, expert and practitioner testimony, online public input (Make.org and Ideas for Change), and in-person ice cream socials.

**Out:** a [final report](https://ct-citizens-assembly.org/media/CTCA-Final-Report.pdf) (October 2026) with ten proposals, all passed by at least two-thirds in the final vote on 10 October:

- **A. Mandate effective date:** new state mandates on towns take effect no earlier than 12 months after enactment, so towns can budget for them.
- **B. Tie education aid to inflation:** the Education Cost Sharing formula rises with the Employment Cost Index.
- **C. Regionalisation of schools:** legislate a plan that incentivises school-district regionalisation and removes barriers to it.
- **D. Consolidate municipal services:** a state-funded commission under the Comptroller to map shared emergency, purchasing, dispatch and administrative services.
- **E. Share restaurant and hotel taxes:** half of the 1% meals tax and 7 of the 15 points of the room occupancy tax go to the host town, for public safety and public works.
- **F. State-funded special education:** the state fully and equitably funds special education costs.
- **G. Third-party oversight of mandates:** an independent review of the fiscal impact of municipal mandates.
- **H. Tax-exemption and PILOT reform:** re-evaluate non-profit property-tax exemptions and payments in lieu of taxes by how accessible and broadly beneficial their services are.
- **I. Millionaire's income tax:** at least an extra 1% on taxable income above $1 million, dedicated to education, childcare and healthcare to offset property taxes.
- **J. Highway tolls:** interstate tolls, with revenue used for property-tax relief, potentially including eliminating the car tax.

## Impact

**Pending.** Comptroller Scanlon pledged to urge lawmakers to give the recommendations a designated public hearing and bipartisan consideration, and said he expects to use the report to drive debate when the 2027 legislative session opens on 6 January. He also said he could not promise to agree with everything the assembly proposed. According to the final report, he is convening a working group of assembly members to present and advocate for the proposals through at least the end of the 2027 session, and legislative leaders from both major caucuses committed to engaging with the assembly. No hearing date has been published yet.

The timing was itself contested. Organisers had scheduled the report so that candidates in the November 2026 election would have to respond to it. Scanlon, who was seeking re-election, asked the assembly to hold its proposals until after polling day; after meeting its governance committee he withdrew the request, and the members went ahead with release on 26 October.

## How it went

As a process, it delivered: a self-governed assembly agreed ten concrete proposals by a two-thirds rule, on a topic professional politicians had avoided. Three lessons stand out.

- **Self-governance can work from day one.** The citizens did not wait to be handed control partway through; they took it at the first session and kept refining the rules, with rotation and lottery guarding against any small group (or the staff) taking over.
- **A perfectly drawn panel is not the panel that shows up.** The lottery panel matched the state within 5.5 points on all thirteen benchmarks. Uneven acceptance (71% of drawn women took a seat against 51% of men; 47% of drawn independents against about two-thirds of Democrats and Republicans) left a visibly skewed room. The report's frank table is a model of transparency other assemblies could copy.
- **Connecting to the macro-public is still early.** Thousands of residents took part online, but well short of the stated ambition of reaching over 10% of the state, and members were wary of AI-synthesised input. Location also limited who could reach the in-person socials.

The real test is still to come: whether the 2027 legislature holds the promised hearing and acts on any of the ten proposals. This report will be updated when it does.
