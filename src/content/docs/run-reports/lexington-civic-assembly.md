title: Lexington Civic Assembly (2026)

**TL;DR.** In March 2026, **Lexington, Kentucky** held its first civic assembly on council pay, accountability and charter review. **CivicLex's current account describes 30 residents**, chosen by [sortition](/concepts/sortition/), meeting across seven sessions. They produced three recommendations, including charter review at least every eight years by lottery-selected residents. Council referred all three proposed amendments to voters on **18 June**; the **3 November 2026** referendum remains ahead.

## What happened

[CivicLex](/ecosystem/overview/) — the Lexington non-profit behind civic education, local-government reporting and [On the Table](/stories/on-the-table/) conversations — convened the city's first civic assembly. Its current [project account](https://civiclex.org/civic-assembly) describes 30 randomly selected residents meeting for 33 hours across seven sessions. Members heard from council members and legal experts, discussed the issues and developed recommendations. Older reporting described 36 members; the current account is the basis for the count used here.

The assembly chose a **70% threshold** for advancing a recommendation. CivicLex reports support of **84.6% for pay**, **88.8% for accountability** and **88.8% for charter review**; it explicitly says none was unanimous.

## What voters will decide

According to [CivicLex's election guide](https://lex.vote/charter-amendments), Council passed the referral ordinance on **18 June 2026**. The guide describes three proposals:

- **Pay:** annual council compensation of **$59,987 in 2026 dollars**, with inflation adjustments, taking effect in **January 2031**.
- **Accountability:** require publicly viewable attendance and accountability expectations for the Council. The detailed standards would be set through a later ordinance.
- **Charter review:** require review at least every **eight years** by residents selected through representative lottery, alongside a permanent advisory body. The periodic resident body and permanent advisory body have different roles.

These are proposals on the **3 November 2026** ballot. As of **20 October**, referral has happened; voter approval has not.

## Why it matters

The assembly proposed making its method recur through charter review, an approach to [institutionalising deliberation](/concepts/institutionalising-deliberation/) that also appeared in [Los Angeles](/run-reports/la-city-charter-assembly/). In Lexington, that proposal has reached voters, while its legal effect remains undecided. CivicLex's Richard Young also argues that an assembly is a useful tool for a difficult decision, rather than a substitute for [durable local civic infrastructure](/concepts/durable-civic-infrastructure/): the everyday reporting, relationships and opportunities to participate that continue between assemblies.

## A participant's view

At the [People Powered × FIDE × GloCAN webinar](https://www.peoplepowered.org/events-content/1000-citizens-assemblies-later-whats-changed) months later, a Lexington participant reflected on the experience. Two threads stood out.

On **process legitimacy over personal agreement** — she disagreed with parts of the assembly's recommendations but felt the room had worked exactly as it should: *"You're not going to agree on everything. This assembly worked exactly the way it was intended to work."* That is a working definition of a legitimate deliberative process — one whose output you can respect even when it isn't the answer you would have written alone.

On the **durable social ties** the room created — months on, she was still in regular contact with three fellow members. One got her a seasonal job at Keeneland racetrack; another couple have dinner with her and her husband. That is the [personal-impact level](/concepts/citizens-assembly-impact-taxonomy/) of the evidence base — usually reported as figures on political efficacy — showing up as ordinary life.
