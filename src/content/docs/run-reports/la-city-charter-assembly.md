title: Los Angeles City Charter Civic Assembly (2026)

---

**TL;DR.** Over four days in early 2026, a randomly-selected panel of Los Angeles residents took on a question put to them by the city's Charter Reform Commission: *what should be the size and structure of the City Council?* Their answer — backed by 89% of delegates — was to expand the Council from 15 to **25 single-member districts** that grow automatically with the population, and to establish a **permanent civic assembly**. Those were recommendations, not enacted changes. Neither proposal, nor the assembly's ranked-choice-voting proposal, appears in the City Clerk's **3 November 2026** voter pamphlet.

| **Charge** | "What should be the size and structure of the Los Angeles City Council?" |
| **Organisers** | Rewrite LA coalition (Public Democracy LA, Metagov, Healthy Democracy, Sortition USA, and others); selection and deliberation designed and run by Healthy Democracy |

| **Outcome** | 9 assembly recommendations, supported by 31 of 35 delegates (89%); several headline proposals are absent from the November ballot |

## What happened

The Los Angeles City Charter — the city's constitution, drafted in 1924 and not substantially changed since 1999 — came under review by a Charter Reform Commission in 2025. Rather than rely on sparsely-attended hearings with one-minute public comments, the Commission signed a Letter of Commitment (October 30, 2025) asking the **Rewrite LA** coalition to convene a civic assembly of everyday Angelenos, and pledged to consider its recommendations.

Delegates were chosen by [civic lottery](/concepts/sortition/): a postcard went to 20,000 residential addresses spread evenly across the city's Council districts — with extra outreach, via social-service organisations, to residents without a fixed address — and a representative panel was drawn at random from respondents to mirror the city by age, gender, race/ethnicity, neighbourhood, party, housing status, and education. Over four days (Feb 28–Mar 1 and Mar 7–8) the assembly heard from 17 expert presenters, surfaced 68 initial policy concepts, and refined them through small- and large-group deliberation into a final slate.

The headline answer to the council-size question: **25 single-member districts**, with the number set to **rise automatically with the census** so that no district exceeds 170,000 residents, and a Council presidency that rotates at random among members. But the delegates went further than the brief. Their first and most striking recommendation was to establish a **permanent, randomly-selected civic assembly** — meeting at least twice a year — with standing power to review, advise on, and refer Council decisions. The rest tightened the city's integrity machinery: ranked-choice voting; an independent, nonpartisan Inspector General to lead the Ethics Commission; removing Council and mayoral appointment power over that Commission; a one-year district-residency requirement; and hiring a professional COO and CFO independent of the mayor and Council.

The Final Report — composed of the delegates' own words — went to the Charter Reform Commission. The Commission submitted its reform package to the City Council in April 2026; that submission did not guarantee that the assembly's proposals would reach voters.

## What reached the November ballot

The [Council's charter-reform file](https://cityclerk.lacity.org/lacityclerkconnect/index.cfm?fa=ccfi.viewrecord&cfnumber=26-0489) records action on the package on **17 June 2026**, finalised on 19 June. The separate [ethics, elections and governance measure](https://cityclerk.lacity.org/lacityclerkconnect/index.cfm?fa=ccfi.viewrecord&cfnumber=26-1100-S13), Charter Amendment EE, received ballot-placement action on **30 June**, finalised on 10 July. Those are procedural decisions, not voter approval.

The [City Clerk's ballot materials](https://clerk.lacity.gov/elections/municipal-elections/ballot-measures) list charter measures on infrastructure and finance (LA), planning (PL), ethics and governance (EE), parks (PRK), proprietary departments (PRT) and school-board campaign-finance rules (SC). The [official voter pamphlet](https://cityclerk.lacity.org/election/ENG101_NOV-2026_Voter_Info_Pamphlet.pdf) contains no measure expanding the Council to 25 districts, creating a permanent civic assembly or introducing ranked-choice voting. EE's Inspector General provisions concern police oversight, a different role from the assembly's proposed Ethics Commission leadership. The ballot package cannot be treated as wholesale adoption of the assembly's recommendations.

As of **20 October 2026**, the **3 November** election remains ahead. The assembly's influence on individual measures, and the eventual vote, need to be tracked separately.

## Why it matters

The Commission's commitment gave this [citizens' assembly](/concepts/citizens-assembly/) a formal route into charter reform, but the ballot shows why consideration and adoption must be distinguished. Asked a narrow question, the assembly proposed [institutionalising deliberation](/concepts/institutionalising-deliberation/) through a standing civic assembly; that proposal has not become a November ballot measure. The convening coalition — [Rewrite LA, Healthy Democracy, Sortition USA, Public Democracy LA and Metagov](/ecosystem/overview/) — also illustrates how civic organisations can bring a deliberative process to a city while elected institutions retain control over what advances.
