**TL;DR.** A citizens' assembly convened across **Victoria and Saanich**, two neighbouring municipalities on Canada's west coast, evaluated by [FIDE North America](/ecosystem/overview/) and facilitated in Canada's long-form "civic lottery" tradition by [MASS LBP](/ecosystem/overview/). It's worth recording less for the topic than for two design details that a well-run assembly shows off in the wild: **concierge-service facilitation** — the care that happens between sessions, not just during them — and the way it arose because a **neighbouring jurisdiction** had already run one.

## At a glance

| | |
|---|---|
| **Where** | Victoria and Saanich, British Columbia, Canada (two adjacent municipalities on southern Vancouver Island) |
| **Selection** | [Sortition](/concepts/sortition/) — a stratified civic lottery, in the Canadian long-form reference-panel tradition |
| **Facilitation** | [MASS LBP](/ecosystem/overview/) — the values-first assembly flow (values → issues → priorities → recommendations) |
| **Evaluation** | [FIDE North America](/ecosystem/overview/) — evaluated against the seven-component framework (see [What is a citizens' assembly](/concepts/citizens-assembly/)) |
| **Recognised gap** | Members felt the assigned mandate wasn't the most applicable to what mattered to them most — an argument for communities having more say over the topic |

## Why it's worth recording

Two things stand out.

### Concierge-service facilitation

Members felt cared for outside the room, not only in it — phone calls between sessions to check they felt heard, personalised follow-up, shared meals, small-group time unrelated to the mandate that still built connection. The tables were set with tablecloths rather than the usual utilitarian conference gear — what one facilitator called "laundry for democracy": a small gesture that communicates *this room, and you in it, is worth care*. These are not garnishes. Practitioners in the tradition treat between-session facilitation as a first-order part of the design, on the theory that a member who feels genuinely welcomed contributes differently from one who feels processed.

For the wider point about the room and its felt qualities, see [spaces for deliberation](/concepts/spaces-for-deliberation/); for the full lifecycle of the craft, [how a citizens' assembly is run](/concepts/running-a-citizens-assembly/).

### The neighbour-effect

The Victoria-Saanich assembly happened in part because a *similar* assembly had already run in the same geographic area. Institutions look sideways: an official whose neighbour just tried something serious will consider it in a way a briefing paper alone rarely triggers. This is the same locality-to-locality pattern documented on the civic-tech side of the wiki — see [locality-to-locality spreading](/concepts/locality-to-locality-spreading/) and, at the level of the movement, [growing the civic-tech field](/concepts/growing-the-civic-tech-field/) and [scaling catalysts](/concepts/scaling-catalysts/). Assemblies spread by neighbours, not by whitepapers.

### An honest limitation

FIDE's evaluation surfaced a specific practitioner tension: some members felt the topic they had been given to work on wasn't the one that felt most pressing in their lives right now. That is a live argument in the field for giving communities more say over the *mandate* — not only over the recommendations — and for the Escobar & Elstub-style [petition-triggered mini-public](/concepts/institutionalising-deliberation/) design where the public sets the agenda.
